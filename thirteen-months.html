Calendar reform · 13 × 28

Thirteen months of twenty-eight days

Every month exactly four weeks, every date on the same weekday every year. It is one of the oldest and most persistent ideas for fixing the calendar, and a large company actually lived by it for six decades. Here is how it works, what today would be, and why the world never switched.

How it works

Thirteen months of 28 days make 364 days, exactly 52 weeks. The Sun's year is about 365.2422 days, so one day (two in leap years) has to live outside the months.

13 equal months

January to June, then a new month, Sol, then July to December. Each has 28 days and starts on a Sunday, so every month looks exactly like the grid in the card above: the 1st is always Sunday, the 28th always Saturday.

Year Day

After 28 December comes Year Day, a holiday that belongs to no month and no week: it is not a Monday or a Saturday, just Year Day. Then 1 January is a Sunday again.

Leap Day

In leap years (the same Gregorian rule: every 4 years, except centuries not divisible by 400) a Leap Day follows 28 June, also outside the week. So the calendar keeps step with the seasons exactly as the Gregorian one does.

What stays fixed

Every date falls on the same weekday forever. Every month has four of each weekday, 20 working days in a Monday-to-Friday week, and the same number of weekends. And every month has a Friday the 13th.

Where the idea came from

  • 1849The philosopher Auguste Comte publishes the Positivist calendar: 13 months of 28 days, each named after a figure he admired, with an extra festival day outside the weeks (and a second one in leap years). It never caught on beyond his followers.
  • 1902Moses B. Cotsworth, a British railway statistician, proposes the 13-month plan with Year Day that becomes known as the International Fixed Calendar. His motive is practical: equal months make one month's figures comparable with the next.
  • 1920sThe League of Nations sets up a committee to study calendar reform. It looks at well over a hundred proposals and treats the 13-month plan as one of the most serious. Cotsworth works with the committee; no reform is adopted.
  • 1928Eastman Kodak adopts the 13-month calendar for its internal accounting, backed by its founder George Eastman, who also financed the campaign for it. Kodak keeps it until 1989 (one account says 1982).
  • 1930Elisabeth Achelis starts promoting a rival, the World Calendar: it keeps 12 months but makes every quarter 91 days and uses the same trick of a blank day outside the week. In the mid-1950s the United Nations considers it and sets it aside, largely over the broken week (below).
  • 1987–1990José and Lloydine Argüelles publish the Dreamspell "13 Moon" calendar: 13 × 28 again, starting the year on 26 July with a "Day Out of Time" on 25 July and no count for 29 February. It is often presented as Maya; it is a modern creation (how it differs).
  • 2012Steve Hanke and Richard Conn Henry propose a permanent calendar that solves the same problem the other way: 12 months, 364 days, and a whole extra week every five or six years, so the seven-day week is never broken.
4

Where it meets other systems

A calendar does not live alone. These are the places where 13 × 28 rubs against the week, the Moon and the calendars people already keep.

The seven-day week

The week has run unbroken for more than two thousand years, independent of any calendar. Year Day and Leap Day are not weekdays, so each one pushes the 13-month calendar's weekdays one step away from the real, continuous week.

That is the single biggest reason the plan failed: a day of rest kept every seventh day (the Jewish Sabbath, the Christian Sunday, the Muslim Friday prayer) would drift across the 13-month weekdays, landing on a different named day every year. Orthodox Jewish groups, Seventh-day Adventists and others opposed it on exactly this point.

The Moon

"13 moons" is a misreading. The Moon's cycle of phases (the synodic month) averages 29.53 days, so a solar year holds about 12.37 of them, not 13. A 28-day month that starts at a new moon ends 1.5 days before the next one, and by the end of the year the months are about 20 days (two-thirds of a lunation) out of step with the phases.

The Gregorian months

Because both calendars use the same year and leap rule, they always agree on 1 January and on the day of the year. Only the month boundaries move: until June a 13-month date comes earlier than the Gregorian date of the same name (1 June is Gregorian 21 May), after Sol it comes later (1 July is Gregorian 16 July), and the two only meet again at the end of the year.

Lunar and lunisolar calendars

The Islamic calendar follows the Moon (12 months, about 354 days) and the Hebrew calendar follows Moon and Sun (12 months, with a 13th month added in 7 years of every 19). Their festivals are counted in their own months, so a solar reform would not move them, but their weekly days of worship would meet the drift described above.

The Hebrew "13-month year" is a different idea altogether: an extra lunar month to stay in step with the seasons, not 13 equal months.

Calendars with fixed months that exist today

Equal months are not just a proposal. The Ethiopian and Coptic calendars have 12 months of 30 days plus a short 13th month of 5 or 6 days, still in daily civil and church use, and they let the ordinary week run straight through the extra days. The French Republican calendar (1793–1805) also had 12 × 30 + 5 or 6, but replaced the week with ten-day décades, one reason it was dropped.

Weeks, quarters and accounts

ISO 8601 week dates already number the year in weeks (52, sometimes 53). Many retailers keep a 13-period year of four-week periods, or a 4-4-5 week quarter, for the same reason Kodak did. But 13 is a prime number: a 13-month year has no clean halves or quarters (each quarter is 3¼ months), which is awkward for anything reported quarterly.

5

For and against

The case for

  • One calendar for every year. A date is always the same weekday, so timetables, school terms, shift rotas and printed calendars never need redoing.
  • Comparable months. Every month has the same number of days and of each weekday, so this month's sales, wages or traffic compare directly with last month's. This is what Kodak used it for.
  • Simple arithmetic. Four weeks a month; the weekday of any date is (day − 1) mod 7 from Sunday. Children could learn it in an afternoon.
  • Predictable holidays. A holiday on a fixed date is on the same weekday every year, so long weekends could be planned for good.
  • Same seasons as now. It keeps the Gregorian year and leap rule, so solstices, equinoxes and the seasons fall exactly where they do today.

The case against

  • It breaks the week. Year Day and Leap Day sit outside the seven-day cycle, which moves days of worship against the named weekdays (see above). This was decisive in the 1930s and 1950s and would be again.
  • Thirteen does not divide. No halves, no quarters, no 3-month seasons: fiscal reporting, tax quarters and many contracts are built on fourths of a year.
  • Every date changes. Birthdays, anniversaries, deadlines, contracts and historical records would all need re-mapping; numeric dates (7/1 is 1 Sol, not 1 July) would be ambiguous for a generation.
  • Days outside the week need rules. Is Year Day a paid day? Does interest accrue? Every legal and payroll system would need an answer.
  • Not lunar either. 28-day months look Moon-like but drift from the real phases just as fast as the Gregorian months do.
  • A Friday the 13th every month, for whoever minds that.

The honest summary: as arithmetic it is cleaner than the Gregorian calendar; as a social object it asks the whole world to re-date everything and to stop the week for one or two days a year. Proposals that keep the week (leap-week calendars) answer the main objection but give up the "one calendar forever" property only partly. None has been adopted.
